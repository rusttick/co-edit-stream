# Data Architecture: AAS Semantics, Content-Addressed History, and Real-Time Collaboration

Purpose: synthesize the AAS/BaSyx, Merkle-DAG/content-addressing, and TerminusDB threads from earlier research into one coherent three-layer architecture, go deep specifically on how real-time multi-user collaboration actually works at the data-structure level, and confirm (with fresh research) the git-like cryptographic content-addressing properties each candidate technology actually has versus merely resembles. STEP and CAD-geometry specifics are deliberately out of scope here per direction. One important correction to earlier research surfaces below: the CRDT library recommended in `possible_web_components.md` (Yjs) is **not** content-addressed, and a different one (Automerge) is — which matters a lot now that "git-like content-addressed crypto features" is an explicit requirement, not just a nice-to-have.

## The three layers, named precisely

1. **Semantic layer** — what the data *means*: the AAS-shaped (or ISO 15926/OML-informed) schema of submodels, properties, and relationships from `possible_agile_manufacturing.md`.
2. **Live collaboration layer** — how multiple people edit the *same* data *simultaneously* without stepping on each other or waiting for a lock.
3. **Durable history layer** — the permanent, content-addressed, cryptographically verifiable record of every accepted state, from `possible_dag_storage`-style research (Merkle-DAG, TerminusDB, Dolt, Fluree, git). This document favors candidates here that combine genuine git-like cryptographic properties with an *open-source* clustering implementation carrying a *clear, documented* CAP position — see the reprioritized recommendation in Layer 3 below, which now leads with Fluree and Dolt rather than TerminusDB.

These are separate concerns solved by separate technology, and conflating them is the most common mistake in this space — AAS says nothing about how multiple people edit data at once, and a CRDT says nothing about long-term durable, queryable, standards-compliant storage. Confirmed directly in this round of research: **the AAS specifications explicitly do not cover information synchronization or caching** — so real-time collaborative editing of AAS data is not something the standard gives you, or even anticipates; it's genuinely open territory you'd be building yourself, not integrating an existing solved piece. Treat any claim otherwise with skepticism.

## Layer 2 in depth: how real-time collaboration actually works, and a corrected recommendation

### The mechanism, precisely

A CRDT (Conflict-free Replicated Data Type) lets multiple replicas of the same document accept concurrent edits independently and later combine them into an identical result on every replica, with no central lock and no negotiation — this is what makes "everyone edits live, all the time, from any workflow-stage view" actually work instead of requiring a single owner or a check-out/check-in model. Both major JS/TS CRDT libraries achieve this, but by structurally different means, and the difference now matters directly to your stated goal:

- **Yjs** — every inserted element gets a unique ID formed from `(clientID, clock)`, a Lamport-timestamp pair, not a cryptographic hash. Its updates are compact `Uint8Array` binary encodings of these `Item` objects; two peers reconcile by exchanging **state vectors** (a set of `(client, clock)` tuples representing "what I've seen") and computing the minimal missing delta. This is fast, mature, and has the largest ecosystem (Hocuspocus, y-websocket, y-sweet) — but **it is not content-addressed**. An update's identity is a position in a per-client counter sequence, not a hash of its content plus its causal dependencies. Two Yjs documents with identical content do not necessarily have identifiers that reflect that.
- **Automerge** — confirmed in this research to be structured internally as a **`ChangeGraph`**: a genuine Hash-DAG where every `Change` (a batch of operations from one transaction) has a **hash computed from its own content plus the hashes of the changes it depends on**, and applying a change requires its dependencies to already be present — exactly the Merkle-DAG property ("any change in a node alters its identifier and all its ascendants") described in the earlier DAG-storage research. Automerge also ships its own **sync protocol** (via `automerge-repo`) explicitly built for both offline-first and live real-time collaboration, using deterministic Lamport-timestamp-plus-actor-ID conflict resolution to guarantee strong eventual consistency.

### The corrected recommendation

`possible_web_components.md` defaulted to Yjs for raw speed and ecosystem size, which was the right call *for that framing* (a generic collaborative diagram editor). Given this conversation's specific, later-stated priority — content-addressed, cryptographically verifiable, git-like history as a first-class requirement, not an afterthought — **Automerge is the better-aligned choice**, because it is *already* a Merkle-DAG of changes at the live-editing layer itself, not something you'd need to bolt content-addressing onto after the fact. The real tradeoff to prototype before committing: Automerge is measurably heavier/slower than Yjs on large documents, so if the live editing surface is genuinely large (a big multi-sheet diagram with thousands of elements being edited by many people at once), that performance gap needs to be measured against your actual scale, not assumed away. For most control-panel/schematic-sized documents this is very unlikely to be the bottleneck; for a whole-factory-scale live graph it might be, and would be worth a real benchmark rather than a guess.

### Why this pairs naturally with the durable layer

Because Automerge's change history is already a hash-linked DAG, committing an accepted, converged state into your durable store isn't a translation step — it's closer to *exporting a slice of the same kind of structure you already have*. This is the practical realization of the "Merkle-CRDT" pattern from the earlier DAG-storage research (Merkle-DAG causal history + CRDT convergent merge, as a named, published design): the live layer and the durable layer are the same *kind* of mathematical object, just with different retention/durability guarantees, rather than two unrelated systems bridged by an ad hoc sync job.

## Layer 3 in depth: content-addressing and git-like crypto properties, confirmed

Fresh research on **TerminusDB's actual internals** (not just its marketing description) confirms it's a legitimate, production-grade match for this layer, not merely git-flavored branding over an ordinary database:

- A TerminusDB database is built from **layers**, each identified by a **content-addressed 20-byte name** — the identifier is a hash of the layer's own content, exactly the property you asked for ("all possible system states are globally and cryptographically uniquely identifiable").
- Each layer is **immutable and append-only on disk**: it records only the additions and deletions (as separate "positive" and "negative" delta graphs) relative to the layer beneath it, never mutating anything already written.
- The base representation uses genuinely **succinct data structures** (front-coded dictionaries, bit sequences, wavelet trees) — benchmarked at roughly **13 bytes per triple at billion-triple scale**, meaning this isn't a toy-scale design; it's built to hold real, large graphs.
- A **commit graph** tracks branch heads; every write produces a commit with an author, timestamp, message, and its own **content-addressed identifier** — i.e., version control semantics (branch, diff, time-travel) are native, not simulated.

### Reprioritized: favoring git-like crypto properties *and* an open-source, CAP-clear clustering story

A closer look at TerminusDB's distributed story (see the companion research this doc supersedes on that specific point) found its multi-node **clustering is Enterprise-only (paywalled)**, and its documentation is silent on partition behavior — no stated CAP position, confirmed or inferable with confidence. That combination (paywalled scaling + unclear CAP story) is disqualifying given the stated priorities here, even though TerminusDB's single-node content-addressing internals are genuine and well-engineered. Reprioritizing the durable layer against two explicit criteria — **git-like content-addressing/crypto properties**, and **an open-source clustering implementation with a clear, documented CAP position** — changes the recommendation:

1. **Fluree** ⭐ — the strongest fit on *both* criteria at once. Its ledger is immutable, time-ordered, and **cryptographically signed block-to-block**, each block linked to its predecessor — genuinely closer to the "git commit as a cryptographic lock" property this whole document is built around than TerminusDB's layer-hashing, because the chaining and signing are the core design, not an internal implementation detail. Clustered deployments use **Raft-based consensus** (with rendezvous hashing to deterministically assign work across nodes) — a well-understood, textbook-documented consensus algorithm, meaning its CAP posture is inferable with actual confidence: Raft requires a quorum to commit, so it is **CP** — it sacrifices write availability during a lost quorum in exchange for real consistency. Licensing is AGPL for the core (open source, not paywalled for clustering), with a commercial license offered only for organizations that specifically want to avoid AGPL's obligations — the same tension already worked through in this project's own licensing discussion, not a hidden feature gate.
2. **Dolt** — the other strong open-source candidate, and unusually transparent about the CAP tradeoff: its clustering and replication **ship in the open-source product** (Apache 2.0), not behind a paywall, and it explicitly offers **two modes** rather than hiding the choice — Direct-to-Standby Replication (built for consistent failover, a CP-leaning choice) and Remote-Based/remote-mediated Replication (built for scaling read load under lag, an AP-leaning choice). Its content-addressing (Merkle-DAG plus "prolly tree" structural sharing) is real and well-documented, though Dolt's native data shape is relational/tabular rather than graph-native, which matters if the semantic layer leans heavily on graph queries rather than tabular ones (BOMs and I/O lists fit Dolt very naturally; deeply nested submodel/relationship graphs less so).
3. **Neo4j** — named here for completeness, not as the recommendation: it has by far the most mature, best-documented distributed CAP story of anything surveyed (Raft-based Causal Clustering, a textbook **CP** system with tunable causally-consistent read replicas, proven at real production scale). It fails the "open-source" criterion specifically — Causal Clustering is Enterprise-only (Community Edition is single-instance, GPLv3) — and it's a property graph, not RDF/AAS-native, costing some of the standards-alignment from the semantic layer. Worth keeping as the benchmark for "what a fully mature version of this looks like" even though it's excluded by the stated criteria.
4. **TerminusDB** and **literal Git / Irmin** move down the list, not off it: TerminusDB remains a reasonable single-node or Enterprise-budget choice if its content-addressing and native graph/WOQL fit outweighs the paywalled-clustering and unclear-CAP concerns for a given deployment; Irmin remains the right tool if the plan is to build a custom distributed system's storage layer from git-like primitives (as Tezos did) rather than adopt a database that already made the consensus decisions — its own CAP posture is whatever gets built on top of it, not something it provides itself.

Either way, the step that makes any of this actually work is **canonicalization before hashing**: if the semantic layer's native form is RDF/OWL, use **RDF Dataset Canonicalization (RDFC-1.0)**; if it stays as AAS's native JSON, use a canonical JSON scheme (RFC 8785 JCS). Pick one representation for the durable layer and hold to it consistently — mixing representations breaks the entire premise that identical content always hashes identically, which is the property this whole layer exists to provide.

## How the standards-interop adapters (from earlier research) fit this architecture, and the backward-compatibility story

The AutomationML, ISA-95/B2MML, eCl@ss/IEC CDD, OSLC, OPC UA, URDF, IFC, and QIF adapters from the earlier interoperability research all target the **semantic layer** (Layer 1) — each one's job is to translate some external tool's native format into the same canonical, AAS-shaped representation everything else in the system uses, and back out again. This is also the concrete backward-compatibility/migration mechanism the earlier discussion was reaching for: **an adapter's output, once canonicalized, becomes just another set of hashed, versioned commits in the durable layer, indistinguishable in kind from anything authored natively in your own app.** A partner's AutomationML export, an ERP's B2MML message, or a vendor's `.aasx` file all end up as ordinary, addressable history once they've passed through canonicalization — nothing about the durable or collaboration layers needs to know or care which external format something originally arrived in. This means adoption doesn't require a hard cutover: legacy-format data and natively-authored data coexist in the same content-addressed history from day one, and an adapter can be added later for any given external format without touching anything already committed.

## Worked example: one edit, through all three layers

Take K3 (the contactor from the earlier AAS walkthrough) again, now tracing the collaboration/history mechanics specifically:

1. Two engineers are simultaneously wiring different parts of the same panel diagram. Each edit is a local Automerge `Change` — hashed, dependent on whatever prior changes that engineer had already seen — and Automerge's sync protocol exchanges these changes live between both sessions (and a server, if one's involved), converging both engineers' views to an identical state within moments, with no lock ever taken.
2. At a natural checkpoint (the engineer marks the sheet ready for review — the WIP→Shared transition from `possible_agile_workflow.md`), the current converged Automerge state is canonicalized (RDFC-1.0 or JCS, whichever representation was chosen) and written as a new content-addressed commit/block into the durable store (Fluree or Dolt, per the reprioritized recommendation above) — this is the moment a live, fluid editing session becomes a permanent, hashed, citable fact.
3. Separately, a partner sends an AutomationML file describing a related piece of equipment. It's run through the AutomationML adapter into the same canonical representation and committed the same way — now sitting in the same content-addressed history, referenceable exactly like the natively-authored data, with no special-casing needed downstream.
4. Six months later, a commissioning tech needs to know exactly what K3's wiring looked like the day it passed FAT: that's a direct lookup of the content-addressed commit tagged as that day's Published state — not a reconstruction, not a guess, a cryptographically verifiable exact answer.

## Honest open questions

- **AAS + CRDT is genuinely unexplored territory.** This research found no existing implementation combining them; you would be building original integration work, not adopting a known pattern. That's not a reason not to do it — it's a reason to prototype the seam (Automerge document ↔ AAS-shaped canonical export) early and cheaply before designing the rest of the system around an assumption that it'll be smooth.
- **Automerge vs. Yjs is a real, measurable tradeoff, not a settled choice.** Prototype both against your actual expected document sizes and concurrent-editor counts before committing — this document's recommendation follows from your stated priority on content-addressing, not from Automerge being unconditionally better.
- **Fluree's and Dolt's ecosystems are both smaller than mainstream databases** (Neo4j, Postgres, etc.) — real operational risk (hiring, community support, long-term maintenance) that should be weighed against how much their native content-addressing, consensus, and branch/merge features are actually saving you versus building similar guarantees yourself on top of a more mainstream store. This risk applies whichever of the two you pick, and applies to TerminusDB too if a given deployment ends up choosing it anyway for its graph/WOQL fit.
- **AGPL is a real, deliberate constraint if Fluree is adopted**, not an oversight — it's the same tension already worked through in this project's own licensing research: fine for an open-source SaaS you intend to keep open, a genuine obligation to weigh if any component built against Fluree is meant to stay closed.
- **Dolt's relational/tabular data shape is a real fit question**, not a formality — verify early whether the semantic layer's actual query patterns (deep relationship traversal across submodels) are well served by a tabular store before committing, rather than assuming a "git for data" story generalizes cleanly from tables to graphs.
- **Canonicalization discipline is a permanent operational requirement, not a one-time setup step.** Every adapter, every export path, and every future contributor to the codebase has to respect the chosen canonical form, forever — a single non-canonical write path silently breaks the "identical content, identical hash" guarantee everything else depends on, and such a bug could go unnoticed for a long time before someone tries to verify history and finds it doesn't check out.
